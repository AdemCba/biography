Rolls-Royce is in the news for a reason that surprises many investors: the shares have fallen hard even though the company has been reporting strong numbers. The stock fell 7.02% on Friday, 23 October, to 1,179 pence, and it has now given back roughly a quarter of its value from the August peak [s:rr-ibtimes]. A Motley Fool UK analysis published around the same time puts the August high at about 1,586p and says the shares plunged 25% to 1,158p on 23 October [s:rr-fool]. The two prices work out to a fall nearer 27%. The exact figures differ between outlets, but the direction is the same.

## Why the shares fell

The main reason is valuation, not operations. The forward price-to-earnings ratio had climbed above 50, and that made some investors ask whether earnings growth could justify the premium [s:rr-ibtimes]. The Motley Fool analysis puts the ratio at about 43.5 times, roughly three times the FTSE 100 average of about 16 [s:rr-fool]. Either way, the stock is priced far above the wider market, so any disappointment is punished.

Technical selling added to the pressure. IBTimes reported that the shares had broken below a 1,405p support level and their 200-day moving average, and that the MACD had shown a bearish crossover [s:rr-ibtimes]. Analyst Anton Kharitonov of Traders Union described "persistent technical weakness" in the chart [s:rr-ibtimes].

Outside the company, the list of worries is long. The Motley Fool piece names rising oil prices, the cost-of-living squeeze, the US midterm elections on 3 November and a possible AI bubble as reasons some investors stepped back [s:rr-fool]. IBTimes adds that average jet fuel prices rose 4.3% in a month, and that security concerns in the Middle East weigh on commercial aviation [s:rr-ibtimes].

## What the results actually show

The business itself is doing well. At the half-year stage, underlying operating profit rose 46% year on year to £2.5 billion and revenue rose 24% to £11.28 billion [s:rr-ibtimes]. The company raised its full-year 2026 guidance to underlying operating profit of £4.7 billion to £4.9 billion and free cash flow of £3.8 billion to £4.0 billion [s:rr-ibtimes].

The guidance raise matters because it makes the sell-off harder to explain on fundamentals alone. The company now expects more operating profit and more free cash flow than it did before the half-year figures [s:rr-ibtimes]. Morningstar also said the recent results did not warrant a higher fair value estimate, a view that suits investors who price shares on models rather than momentum [s:rr-ibtimes].

The divisions show where the growth is. Civil Aerospace operating profit was up 31%, Defence rose 57% and Power Systems rose 72% [s:rr-fool]. Power Systems is tied to the data centres that power AI, which is why the same theme that supports the stock can also make it vulnerable if confidence in the AI boom weakens [s:rr-fool].

## Why some investors are buying again

Not everyone is selling. The Motley Fool report notes that Rolls-Royce was the most-bought stock on the AJ Bell platform in the previous week, at almost 5% of purchases [s:rr-fool]. That suggests retail investors see the fall as a chance rather than a warning.

The Motley Fool author, who owns shares in the company, calls Rolls-Royce "well worth considering" for a balanced FTSE 100 portfolio, but says it is not a bargain at current levels and that he is not buying more [s:rr-fool]. That view sums up the split. The business is strong; the price already reflects much of that strength.

## What to watch next

Three things will decide the next move. The first is the price-to-earnings ratio: if profit growth continues at the pace of the first half, the premium may look more reasonable over time. The second is defence spending, because the same report warns that tight government budgets in the West could limit demand [s:rr-fool]. The third is the power business, which depends on AI investment. Small modular reactor profits are still years away, though the company has won European tenders, including in Sweden [s:rr-fool].

The US midterms on 3 November will also shape sentiment for the wider market, and therefore for a stock with a valuation this high. For investors, the question is no longer whether Rolls-Royce is a good company, but how much growth is already built into the price.
